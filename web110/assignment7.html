The lesson on computer networks opened up my eyes to many small details that come together for a computer to be connected to the internet, and what even being connected to the internet means. When I worked on reviewing my own IP address settings for my network, I learned that a device receives its address from its router. I learned how to figure out my own IP address's details and what numbers represented the IP address's street and house number. When I told my laptop to give its address back and get a new one it came back with the same address again. Understanding the building blocks and how to navigate the understanding of your network connection is beneficial in the IT world because checking the IP address is usually the first thing to troubleshoot when someone says they can't connect. If a computer doesn't have a proper address, then the issue would come from the connection to the router, not the computer's programs.

As I worked through and explored my own internet connectivity, I learned about the ping commands in the Command Prompt. A ping command sends a small message to a device, and times how long it takes to get a reply. My router took about 16 milliseconds. I was surprised to see that my router's replies jump around from 3-28 milliseconds even though the computer stayed in the same house. This happened because the Wi-Fi isn't a cable connected to my computer, so it varies in speed and is less steady. I liked playing with the tracert command because it breaks down the actual path that my data took to reach Google. It went through 11 stops before actually reaching Google. This showed me that the internet is really just a long chain of connected networks. In a tech job this would help me figure out if a problem is coming from my computer's end or if it's from the server end.

I used to think that my router is just a box that connected me to the internet. But through the activity to look into the components of my home network from this week's lesson, I learned that it's several pieces doing different jobs. The modem connects my home to my internet provider, Comcast. The router gives each device in my home its own address and lets them all share one internet connection. The Wi-Fi is what sends my connection through the air so that my laptop and phone don't need to rely on cables for internet connection. My Wi-Fi's security is WPA2, which seems like the safe standard. Older security called WEP is no longer safe because it can be broken. When I think about applying this information to the real world, I think about how if someone is working from home and they say their internet is slow I would know where to begin checking, and that restarting the modem before the router usually works best.
